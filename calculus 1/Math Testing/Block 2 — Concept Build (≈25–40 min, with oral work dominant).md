Suggested script:

> “Today I’m reviewing limits from §2.2 and §2.3 of Stewart. A limit $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)$ is the single number that $f(x)$ gets closer and closer to as $x$ gets close to $a$, without caring what happens at $x=a$ itself.
> 
> A one-sided limit, like $\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)$ or $\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)$, looks only from the left or the right. The two-sided limit exists only if both one-sided limits exist and are equal.
> 
> Discontinuities at a point come in a few important flavors. A **removable** discontinuity happens when the limit exists, but $f(a)$ is missing or has the wrong value—graphically, a hole in an otherwise nice curve. A **jump** discontinuity happens when the left and right limits exist but are different heights, so there’s a vertical jump in the graph. There are also **infinite** discontinuities where the function blows up toward $\infty$ or $-\infty$, and **oscillatory** ones like $\sin(1/x)$ near 0, where the graph wiggles forever without settling.
> 
> For rational functions, if I can factor and cancel, I usually get a hole (removable). If I can’t cancel the zero in the denominator, I often get a vertical asymptote and an infinite limit. My job as a student is to (1) decide if the left and right limits match, (2) decide if the function value agrees with the limit, and then (3) clearly state whether the limit exists and what type of discontinuity I see.”

Plain-language anchors to emphasize while you speak:

1. “A limit is about what values get _close to_, not what the function _equals_ at the point.”
    
2. “Two-sided limit exists ⇔ left limit equals right limit and both are finite numbers.”
    
3. “Discontinuity type depends on _how_ the graph misbehaves: hole, jump, blow-up, or endless wiggle.”

### Definitions

- limit $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)$: The single value $f(x)$ approaches as $x$ gets arbitrarily close to $a$, if such a value exists.
    
- one-sided limit: A limit taken only from one direction, $\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)$ (left) or $\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)$ (right).
    
- continuity at a point: $f$ is continuous at $a$ when $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)$ exists and equals $f(a)$.
    
- removable discontinuity: The limit exists at $a$ but $f(a)$ is missing or not equal to that limit (a “hole fixable by redefining the point”).
    
- jump discontinuity: Left and right limits both exist but are unequal, producing a vertical jump in the graph.
    
- infinite discontinuity: As $x\to a$, values of $f(x)$ grow without bound ($\pm\infty$), often at a vertical asymptote.
    
- oscillatory DNE: The function values near $a$ keep oscillating between different values without settling to one number, so no limit exists.
